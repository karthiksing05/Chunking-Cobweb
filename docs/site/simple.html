Research summary · 19 November 2026

TRELLIS: learning language and chess from concepts and chunks

A learner that reads a stream one item at a time, with no neural network and no gradients, and still learns to parse, generate and remember. Here is what it is, what it can do, and what is still open.

The idea in one picture

People compress what they experience in two ways. They chunk: parts that keep occurring together get stored as one unit ("once upon a time", a castled king). And they generalize: things that behave alike get grouped into a concept ("dog", "cat" and "bird" can all be the subject of "ran").

TRELLIS does both, and each one feeds the other. Concepts give chunks their vocabulary, and chunks give concepts something bigger than single words to group.

Both hierarchies are Cobweb trees, which grow one example at a time. Nothing is trained offline and no labels are used; gold labels are only for scoring.

What it does with them

Parse
Given a sentence, find the most likely way to bracket it into chunks, for example ((the bird) (looked sad)).
Generate
Write a new sentence one word at a time. A general concept ("animal") is narrowed by the context to one word, which is how it says things it has never seen.

TinyStories

Parsing: telling grammatical sentences from broken ones

We took real sentences and broke them in controlled ways, then asked whether TRELLIS accepts each one. A good learner accepts the real sentences and rejects the broken ones.

real: the fish was not happy anymore
scrambled: not anymore was happy the fish
two words swapped: little the bird looked sad
agreement error: they is not friends anymore
content swapped (grammatical, nonsense): the hard door looked hard

What we found:

  • The parse tree alone works like a syntax checker. It accepts the content-swapped nonsense (90%) as readily as real sentences, because the grammar is fine. It is weak against scrambles.
  • The generator's word probabilities do the real work. With them, TRELLIS keeps up with the n-gram, LSTM and GPT baselines at 125 to 500 sentences on scrambles, swaps and agreement errors.
  • It falls behind as data grows, and it is weakest on meaning: at 4,000 sentences it still accepts 63% of content-swapped sentences, where the neural models accept about 40%.
Chess

Memory like a chess player's

In 1973 Chase and Simon showed people a chess board for five seconds and asked them to rebuild it. Masters recalled far more than beginners, but only on real positions; on random boards the advantage disappeared. The explanation was chunks: masters see familiar groups of pieces.

TRELLIS, given a short-term memory of a few chunks, reproduces that pattern:

Pieces recalled, middle gameBeginnerClub playerMaster
Chase & Simon (people)4816
TRELLIS, 7 chunks of memory, after 300 / 1,000 / 4,000 positions7.29.712.9

With more experience it reaches the master's 16 pieces at about 8,000 positions and levels off near 17. On random boards its recall stays flat. The chunks it learns are what players would name: pawn chains, batteries, and castled kings. The most common one is the castled king with its pawn shield, f2 g1 g2 h2.

Open question

Does the level of abstraction control what it writes?

The hypothesis: by changing one setting, how abstract TRELLIS's word categories are, we should get three kinds of output.

SettingExpected outputExampleReached?
Exact wordsGrammatical and meaningfulhe liked to play with his ballYes (63%)
Middle categoriesGrammatical but meaninglessthe hard door looked hardNot yet (at most 26%)
No categoriesWord saladgive it is shinyYes (over 90%)

The middle case is hard. With only about 240 different words in 1,000 children's sentences, nearly any swap of one noun for another still makes sense ("a big cat came down from the sky"). And TRELLIS's categories group words by meaning as well as by grammar. We are now testing it on 4,000 sentences, where there are more words to go wrong with.

Chess does not show it either: TRELLIS recognises familiar positions but does not know the rules, so it cannot yet produce "legal but pointless" moves on purpose.

In short

  • A learner built only from two growing concept trees can parse, generate and remember, with no gradients.
  • With little data it writes as well as small neural networks, and its chess memory looks like a human expert's.
  • It falls behind neural models as data grows, and it is weaker on meaning than on grammar.
