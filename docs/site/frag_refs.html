Simple reference generators, run on the same stream, prompts and judge, produce more new grammatical sentences than TRELLIS. At 1,000 sentences TRELLIS's candidate recipe yields .224 per generation; the trigram sampler yields .400, the one-word swap on a retrieved sentence .571, and the best reference (One-word swap, retrieved sentence) .571, 2.5 times TRELLIS. TRELLIS's errors come from word pairs it never saw next to each other. This page reports the references, the split of TRELLIS's errors and what follows from it.

What worked. Two Cobweb trees over word types, read by counting each member word once, decide which unseen word pairs may be produced far better than the learner's context concepts do (Table 5). A generator that replaces one word of a retrieved stored sentence, and accepts a replacement only when two readings of those trees agree, produces sentences that all contain a never-seen word pair and are about as often grammatical as the best reference, which never leaves stored pairs (Table 6). The same trees shorten the code for held-out sentences (Table 7). None of this is inside the learner yet: it reads the stream, one word at a time, and the learner's chunks did not help as units.

How to read this page

Yield is the number of judged-grammatical new sentences per generation. Cross share is the topic agreement with the previous sentence, defined as in A18. A word sequence is stored when it occurs in the stream the learner saw. The references never see the learner; they read only the stream.

A replacement stored between the same two neighbours on each side gives the most grammatical output and the fewest new sentences; one neighbour on each side gives more new sentences at a lower rate; two swaps in a row, or a replacement licensed only by its two stored pairs, cost grammaticality.

Analogy count: for an unstored pair (a, b), the number of stored pairs (a2, b2) such that (a, b2) and (a2, b) are also stored. Two trees by side: each word type is described by the set of words stored just after it in one tree and just before it in the other; a word weighs as its count; a new concept is scored by its own expected correct guesses (exact_alone). The licence for (a, b) is the share of the words grouped with a that b followed, summed over the concepts on a's path, combined with the same reading for b's side.

  • The current learner generalizes about neighbours no better than word frequency. Every recipe variant we could score ranks unstored pairs about as well as multiplying the two words' frequencies. This is the measured cause of the errors in Table 3.
  • Three changes were each needed. Describing word types by their neighbours, instead of occurrences by their windows, helps a little when the tree is read by token counts. Reading the same tree by types, each member word counting once, helps much more: one frequent collocation no longer stands for its whole group. Splitting the description into one tree per side reaches the analogy count. A tree over word pairs does worse than word frequency.
  • It fits one-pass learning and grows with data. Kept current one occurrence at a time, the two trees score the same as when built at the end. They pass the analogy count at 2,000 and 4,000 sentences.
  • exact_alone is required. Without it the word-type tree stays nearly flat (a mean path of about three concepts) and ranks pairs no better than word frequency.

Frame votes: a candidate word is scored by how often the words grouped with it, in the two word-type trees, stood in the place around the slot; the candidate itself counts where it stood there. Best slot: every position offers its best candidate, weighed by that candidate's votes relative to the votes of the word standing there, and the highest ratio is taken. The references and rounds 119 to 121 read only the stream; round 123 runs the same procedure on the learner's memory. Pair licence: the two-tree licence of Table 5 for a pair of neighbouring words. Frame votes times pair licence: a replacement that makes an unstored pair is scored by its frame votes multiplied by the pair licence of each unstored pair it makes, so it must be supported by both readings of the trees.

  • Take the best candidate; do not draw. Drawing a replacement in proportion to its votes is grammatical about a quarter of the time: most of the vote mass lies on many weakly supported words.
  • Choosing the slot matters as much as choosing the word. Letting every position offer its best replacement and taking the best offer beats the reference swap on yield. Most of that gain is reuse: few of its outputs contain an unstored pair.
  • One reading of the trees is not enough to generalize. When an unstored pair is required, frame votes alone or the pair licence alone are grammatical 39 to 52% of the time. The analogy count, which ranked pairs well in Table 5, is grammatical 10 to 16% of the time: its best-scoring pairs are pairs of frequent words.
  • Two readings that agree generalize as well as reuse. Scoring a replacement by its frame votes times the pair licence of each unstored pair it makes gives 67 to 68% grammatical output at 1,000 sentences and 59 to 61% at 4,000, with every output new and containing a never-seen pair. The reference swap, which makes no unstored pair, is 69% and 67% grammatical. Yield is .67 to .68 against .57 at 1,000 sentences and .59 to .61 against .51 at 4,000. With the best slot and no requirement, the outputs that contain an unstored pair are at least as often grammatical as those that do not.
  • The learner's chunks do not help as units. Running the same procedure on the learner's memory with its committed chunks as units gives equal or lower yield than words alone (round 123 rows). Its parses are weak (A18), so its chunks are poor units to substitute.

The judge passes 86 to 91% of real held-out sentences, so about 68% is still short of real text. The topic comes from retrieval: these generators inherit the cross share of the stored sentence they start from.

What follows

  • For the learner. Its context tree describes occurrences by their windows. The licence that works describes types by their sets of neighbours, one tree per side, and reads concepts by types. Moving this into the learner is the next step; src/trellis2/type_trees.py holds the mechanism.
  • For generation. Chart generation draws every word from smoothed distributions and pays for it in unstored pairs. Substitution on a retrieved analysis keeps what is stored and generalizes only where two readings agree. It has no cut, no smoothing weight and no topic weight.
  • For chunks. The frame votes are a substitution test: a span that stands where single words stand, and is voted for by their groups, is a unit. Using that test to find chunks has not been tried.

What this does not settle

  • Everything here replaces one word. Two or more substitutions, and units longer than a word, are open.
  • Stories only. Characters and chess have not been run with these trees.
  • The coder's half-and-half mix is hand-set.
  • Rounds 110 and 119 to 123 are judged on fewer seeds where the tables say so.
