## 1. Subjects (CS73a, p. 57, Method)

- N = 3. A master (M), a Class A player (A), and a beginner (B). Ratings are not given. (The acknowledgement on p. 55 credits Hans Berliner as subject; the paper does not say he is M, so treat that as not stated.)
- Everything is a case study of one subject per skill level. All "standard errors" in the paper are across positions (n = 5), not across subjects.

## 2. Stimuli (CS73a, pp. 57-58)

- 20 games taken from chess books and magazines, played by "advanced players (masters, experts, and perhaps a few Class A players)".
  - 10 middle-game positions at about White's 21st move, **24-26 pieces** on the board.
  - 10 end-game positions at about the 41st move, **12-15 pieces**.
  - Not all positions were "quiet": some caught an exchange in progress (p. 58; also p. 61).
- 8 random positions: 4 from middle games and 4 from end games, "by taking actual positions and replacing the pieces randomly on the board" (p. 58). The paper gives no further detail on the randomization (uniform over squares, legality constraints, etc.). Not found.
- Total 28 positions ("One of the 28 chess positions was set up", p. 58).
- Per subject, per task (p. 58): 5 middle-game, 5 end-game, 2 random middle-game, 2 random end-game = 14 positions per task. The paper does not explicitly say that the 10+10 game positions were split 5/5 between the two tasks. That is an inference from 5+5 per task. The same positions may have been used in both tasks; not stated.
- Follow-up puzzle set (p. 62): 9 new positions chosen at random per subject from Reinfeld (1945) "Win at chess" (tactical but not mid-exchange, from actual master games), one 5-s trial each.
- Chess relations a-priori probabilities were computed over 30 positions (game) and 8 random positions (footnote 4, p. 68), about 12,606 piece pairs (p. 68).

## 3. Perception (copy) task (CS73a, p. 58)

- Two boards side by side about 6 in apart. The stimulus position was set up on the subject's left. An empty board stood directly in front of him. A full set of pieces was to the right of the blank board.
- A partition hid the left board. When removed, the subject reconstructed the position on the blank board "as quickly and accurately as possible, glancing at the position on the left as often as he wished."
- Behaviour recorded on videotape. Analysis (p. 59): each piece placement was logged with the time, to 1/10 s, between that placement and the next.
- Segmentation:
  - Within-glance interval: between two pieces placed without looking back at the stimulus.
  - Between-glance interval: two pieces separated by a glance back at the original position (p. 62).
  - Assumption (p. 57): the subject encodes **one chunk per glance** while reconstructing. Pieces placed after a single glance = one chunk (hypothesis 1, p. 64).
- Caveats the authors state (p. 70): no guarantee against more than one chunk per glance. Subjects reported glancing often and retaining little. For M there was no evidence of perseveration; most of his glances, including head movement and time to place the next piece, were close to 2 s and almost none were over 4 s.
- Pieces per glance in middle games (p. 75): M 2.0, A 2.8, B 2.0.
- Footnote 3 (p. 63): the authors say they replicated the experiment as an oral task (pointing to squares and naming pieces) and as a paper-and-pencil task without altering the results, to be reported later. Those results are not in this paper.

## 4. Memory (recall) task (CS73a, p. 58)

- Same board layout and apparatus as the copy task. Partition removed, **5 s** view, partition replaced. Then free recall by placing pieces on the front board, "as much time as he wished (subjects rarely took more than 1 min)."
- Multiple trials per position (unlike de Groot): if the reconstruction was not perfect, the front board was cleared and another 5-s view plus recall was run. Trials continued until perfect recall, "except for the random positions, which were too difficult to continue to criterion". The number of trials run on random positions is not stated (Figs 1-2 x-axes run to 7 trials; not read).
- Score: number of pieces on the correct square, first trial (primary) and on later trials (p. 59). Error and omission counts were not reported; the paper only says errors mostly come late in the protocol with long latencies and few relations (p. 72).
- No indication of who was on move; board always viewed from White's side (footnote on p. 62). de Groot told subjects who was on move and gave the mover's view.

## 5. Chunk definition and relations analysis

### 5.1 Definition (CS73a pp. 59, 62-65)

- Five relations recorded between **successively placed** pieces: attack (A, either attacks the other), defense (D, either defends the other), proximity (P, on one of the 8 adjacent squares), same color (C), same type (S). 16 combinations of A/D/P/C/S (D implies C, so the 32 raw combinations collapse to 16).
- Hypothesis (p. 64): about 2 s is needed to recognize a chunk and store a label for it in STM; successive elements of a chunk follow in hundreds of ms (cited Dansereau 1969: ~2 s to begin a chunk, ~300 ms per further element). A **pause of >= 2 s (strictly "less than (or equal to) 2 s" vs "greater than 2 s" in Table 5, p. 73) marks a chunk boundary** in the memory task. Short pauses are inside a chunk. The authors say (p. 65) that using 2.5 s instead of 2 s would not change results.
- Within-glance intervals "seldom exceeded 2 sec", modal intervals 0.5 s or less (p. 62). Very short intervals (<= 0.5 s) are mostly cases where several pawns or rooks were picked up at once; it took at least 1 s to retrieve a piece from the side of the board (p. 63).
- Between-glance mean latencies (perception task): **M 2.8, A 3.2, B 3.5 s** (differences p < .05, pooled error term) (p. 62).
- Recall-phase vs reconstruction-phase criterion (p. 76): reconstruction phase = a pause of **>= 10 s** followed by mostly errors, or a series of pauses **>= 5 s** with errors. Chunks beyond that are excluded from the chunk-count analysis (Table 8).
- In Table 9 a "chunk" is two or more pieces within 2 s (p. 79).

## 6. Key human results (CS73a)

### 6.1 Accuracy, pieces correct on the first 5-s trial (p. 59-61, text only; Figures 1-2 not read)

| Position type | M | A | B |
|---|---|---|---|
| Middle game | about 16 | about 8 | about 4 |
| End game | about 8 | about 7 | about 4 |
| Random (middle and end) | not found (figures) | not found | not found |

- Random positions: "no relation at all between memory of the position and playing strength", and first-trial performance of all three on random positions was "even poorer than B's performance on the actual game positions" (p. 60-61). Numbers were not in the text. GS96a Table 1 (p. 20) lists the CS73a random cell values, marked "estimated from graph": **3.0, 2.5, 2.0** pieces across the skill columns <1600 / 1600-2000 / >2350. Read as B, A, M, which gives the master the lowest score; GS96a (p. 6-7) notes CS73a is the only one of 13 experiments where the stronger player did worse. Treat as estimated.
- Trials to perfect recall, middle games: M 3-4; A 1-2 more than M; B many more, up to 14 in one case. End games: M 2-3, A 3-4, B 4-7 (p. 59-60).
- Later-trial gains: M adds about 4 pieces per trial after trial 1; A and B 5-6 (p. 59).
- Standard errors on first trial (5 positions) are plotted but values are not in the text.
- Puzzle positions, one 5-s trial, percent correct (p. 62): B 33, A 49, M 81, versus first trial on the original positions: B 18, A 34, M 62. de Groot's masters typically placed 23-24 of 25 pieces correctly (p. 61). The authors attribute M's lower score to using positions that were not quiet and from lesser players.
- M recognized 4 of the 9 puzzle positions within the first second of exposure, yet his recall was the same for recognized vs unrecognized positions: 83% vs 79% (p. 62).

### 6.2 Latencies (pp. 62-64)

- Within-glance intervals seldom > 2 s, mode <= 0.5 s. Between-glance means: M 2.8, A 3.2, B 3.5 s (p. 62).
- Memory task: numerous intervals up to about 2.5 s, then a tail (p. 63). Distributions across tasks "not dissimilar" (Fig. 4, not read).
- GS98 (p. 24 of its preprint) read CS73a's within-glance data from the graph: about 80% of within-glance latencies under 2 s, median about 1 s, mode about 0.5 s.

## 7. CHREST / MAPP simulations (secondary)

### 7.1 MAPP (Simon & Gilmartin 1973): original not read

What later papers say (G98 preprint pp. 10-11; G93 pp. 463-464):
- MAPP combines PERCEIVER (Simon & Barenfeld 1969 eye-movement model) and EPAM. Chunks are identified through an EPAM-style discrimination net (a **binary tree**, G93), and pointers to identified chunks go into a limited-size, **static** STM (7 +/- 2 chunks, unordered). Same STM limit and encoding rate as other domains.
- MAPP "simulated several experimental findings, including the percentage of pieces recalled by a class A player, the types of pieces replaced and the chess relations between successive pieces in the reconstruction" (GS98 p. 5). Its results were quantitatively inferior to masters but superior to Class A players. Qualitatively it placed the same groups of pieces as human players (G98).
- Extrapolation: grandmaster performance would need a repertoire of **10,000 to 100,000** configurations in LTM, usually quoted as 50,000 (G98 p. 11; GS98 p. 5).
- Known failure (G93 p. 463): MAPP could not produce the number of chunks an expert places (more than 7 +/- 2) and could not explain resistance of recall to interfering material.
- Unverified, from a web-search summary of the abstract and not from a document I opened: ~73% accuracy; 4.4-5.75 patterns per position; 2.45-3.9 pieces per pattern; LTM of about 2,000 nodes. Do not quote these without checking Simon & Gilmartin directly.

### 7.2 Gobet (1993) CHREST (CogSci 15, pp. 463-468)

- Learning phase: 20 fixations per position (arbitrary), STM set to 20 items during learning to encourage redundant chunks. 2,843 positions (230 from Hort & Jansa 1980 plus games from a personal database). Net had **3,646 nodes** at the end; redundant nodes 4 after 100 positions, 10 after 1,000, 14 after 2,000, 20 at the end.
- Test: STM 7 chunks, 20 fixations (about 5 s, from de Groot 1980). Recall test after every 10 learning positions with 2 game and 2 random positions. Mean of the last 100 tests: **10.66 pieces (sd 1.08) for game positions and 7.77 (sd .65) for random**; t(99) = 21.9. The author notes model random recall is higher than human random recall (about 4 pieces).
- Multi-board recall (1-5 boards, 5 s each): three snapshots at 230, 1,538 and 2,843 positions learned (630, 2,175, 3,646 nodes). Model accounts for 97.4% of the Class A players' results in G&S experiment 1b and 77.5% in experiment 3.

### 7.3 Gobet & Simon (1996, M&C) CHREST simulation: random-position recall vs number of chunks learned (GS96c pp. 8-10)

- Simplified CHREST. Learning source: several thousand positions from recent grandmaster games. Learning phase: 20 random fixations per position, pieces within 2 squares of the fixated square sorted through the net, patterns stored with locations (e.g. short castle [Pf2, Pg2, Ph2, Kg1, Nf3]).
- Test: 50 new positions each in four orientations (normal, horizontal, vertical, central reflections). Tested after each 10,000 nodes added (more often early on), net up to **70,000 nodes** (14 nets). Learning halted during tests. 20 random fixations per test (equated to about 5 s, De Groot & Gobet in press). Score: percent of stimulus pieces appearing in the right place in at least one recognized chunk (erroneous placements not penalized).
- Results: average over the 14 nets, normal 65.4% vs horizontal 63.2%, vertical 53.3%, central 52.5%. Percentage = a + b log(number of nodes) fits all four conditions with more than 98% of the variance. **Recall of random positions improves slightly with nodes, up to 23.4%.** Delta (normal+horizontal minus vertical+central) averages 11.4%, rising until the fourth net (about 2,500 chunks) then stable.
- GS96a (p. 9) summarizes: "the program's recall of random positions increased logarithmically as a function of the number of chunks that had been learned through the study of game positions."

### 7.4 Gobet & Simon (1996, PBR): skill effect with random positions in humans (GS96a pp. 6-8, 20-22)

ANOVA on skill with experiment as block: F(3,17) = 10.35, MSe = .97, p < .001. Reconstruction mode (board/dictation/computer) had no effect, F(2,27) = .90.

  - Saariluoma 1985 exp 3 (9, 5 s): 2.6, 3.8, 4.6.

Random-position percent correct was shown only in Fig. 4: not found as a number.

### 7.5 Gobet & Simon (2000), CHREST with templates, presentation times 1 to 60 s (GS00 web print pp. 7-24)

**Human experiment**
- 21 subjects ran; one quit; 20 analyzed: Masters n = 5 (mean Elo 2498), Experts n = 8 (2121), Class A n = 7 (1879). Ages 20-70 (mean 32.9).
- Materials: 19 game positions (about move 20, White to move, quiet, from obscure master games, mean 25 pieces), 10 random positions made by assigning pieces from a game position to random squares. Computer display (Macintosh SE/30), reconstruction by mouse. 2 s black screen before the blank board. 2 warm-up positions (5 s).
- Presentation times 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 10, 20, 30, 60 s: two game positions and one random position per time (Masters did not get game positions over 10 s). Order ascending for about half of each group, descending for the others.
- Fit P = 100 - B exp(-c (t - 1)), (100 - B) = percent recalled after 1 s (Table 1, p. 9):
  - Game: Class A 100-B = 24.8, c = .033; Experts 33.6, c = .074; Masters 70.8, c = .435 (Masters r2 = .35 on all points).
  - Random: Class A 9.4, c = .006; Experts 14.7, c = .012; Masters 19.5, c = .018.
  - Masters averaged 92.4% at 10 s on game positions (two Masters about 85%, three above 96%).
  - Random positions differ by skill at short times (<= 10 s) F(2,15) = 7.74, p < .005 and at long times F(2,16) = 14.36, p < .001.
- Chunks (2-s corrected latency rule): number of chunks Masters 2.9 at 1 s, 4.2 at 4 s, 3.3 at 10 s; Experts 2.2 at 1 s, 4.5 at 10 s, 2.9 at 60 s; Class A 2.2 at 1 s to 5.4 at 60 s (p. 12). Largest chunk for Experts 9-10 pieces at 1 s rising to about 17 at 60 s; Class A 4-5 at 1 s (pp. 10-11).

**CHREST model** (pp. 16-19)
- Components: LTM discrimination net, **STM capacity three visual chunks** (queue, except the largest chunk seen so far, the "hypothesis", which stays until a larger one is met), mind's eye, eye-movement module.
- Each fixation delineates a visual field of +/- 2 squares. Pieces in it are sorted through the net.
- Time parameters: **discrimination (new node/branch) 8 s; familiarization (add to existing chunk) 2 s; filling a template slot 250 ms; encoding a chunk into STM 50 ms** (pp. 17-18).
- Learning phase: scan thousands of master-game positions with simulated eye movements. Three nets sized by matching the 5-s human game and random recall of the three skill groups: **500 nodes (Class A), 10,000 (Experts), 300,000 (Masters)** (p. 19). Two degrees of freedom per net were consumed in that fitting.
- Test: for each presentation time, 50 game positions (not used in learning) and 50 random positions made as in the human study. Reconstruction places STM contents first, then LTM. Template POS contents are placed as one chunk. Conflicts are resolved by placement frequency.

**Fits (pp. 19-23)**
- Simulation parameters, game positions (Table 3): 500 nodes 100-B = 30.2, c = .018; 10k nodes 44.0, c = .062; 300k nodes 54.9, c = .287. Random: 500 nodes 12.9, c = .008; 10k 16.2, c = .010; 300k 18.1, c = .013. All r2 > .95. Both parameters rise with skill like the human data, except human Masters on game positions (model underestimates; the human (100-B) = 70.8 vs model 54.9).
- Number of chunks (Table 4): model is below 3 at short times and increases with time; model misses the human "bulge" (increase then decrease) for Masters and Experts. Human vs simulated mean number of chunks, game: A 3.4 vs 3.0; E 3.1 vs 2.6; M 3.4 vs 2.1. Random: A 2.0 vs 2.3; E 2.4 vs 2.8; M 2.5 vs 2.9.
- Largest chunk mean (Table 4), game: A 7.6 vs 5.5; E 12.3 vs 13.5; M 14.5 vs 19.6. Random: A 4.4 vs 3.0; E 5.9 vs 5.2; M 6.6 vs 4.9.
- Errors of omission (Table 5), game, human vs sim: A 9.5 vs 12.5; E 5.8 vs 6.2; M 0.6 vs 1.3. Random: A 16.9 vs 18.2; E 14.6 vs 14.3; M 13.6 vs 14.2. Commission, game: A 4.5 vs 1.7; E 4.2 vs 2.8; M 2.2 vs 2.8. Random: A 3.5 vs 1.6; E 3.7 vs 4.6; M 2.1 vs 3.2. The authors note commission errors are the weak fit.
- Template ablation (300k net, no template-slot access, percent correct on 50 game positions at 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 10, 20, 30, 60 s): 58.4, 64.9, 64.1, 64.3, 63.5, 64.5, 80.7, 88.9, 99.1. With templates the model reaches 84% at 10 s (their Fig. 5), against human Masters at 85% to above 96% at 10 s (p. 23).
- Stated limitation (p. 24): the program underestimates Masters' game recall; the authors suggest more nodes or templates.

### 7.7 Revalidation of the Chase & Simon relations analysis (GS98)

- Subjects: 26 (Masters n = 5 mean Elo 2453, Experts n = 9 mean 2148, Class A n = 12 mean 1869) (GS98 p. 10 of the preprint).
- Mouse-and-screen copy and recall. Mouse movement time was subtracted from latencies (corrected).
- Corrected latency: 79.5% of within-glance latencies under 2 s (median 1.37 s, mode 1.13 s), versus 1.11% of between-glance; under 2.5 s: 89.3% vs 4.4%. Between-glance median 7.3 s (CS73a means about 3 s).
- A-priori relation probabilities from 100 positions and 26,801 pairs; null relation .335; AS = .001.
- Correlation of their Table 2 with CS73a Table 7: r = .78 (61% of variance). Within-chunk deviation from a-priori correlated with the number of relations: copy game .81, copy random .68, recall game short latencies .86, recall random .79. Between-chunk: copy game .61 (others not read).
- They conclude CS73a underestimated chunk size; the CS73a master's largest median chunk was 5 pieces (first chunk mean 3.8, A 2.6, B 2.4). They suggest the physical constraint of grasping pieces limited chunk size in 1973. Their Masters studied the stimulus for tens of seconds before placing the first piece, a different strategy from CS73a's glance cycles.

## 8. Exact-replication requirements for a computational model

1. Stimulus set: 10 middle-game (24-26 pieces, about move 21) and 10 end-game (12-15 pieces, about move 41) positions from advanced-player games, some mid-exchange; plus 4 randomized middle-game and 4 randomized end-game positions (same pieces, random squares). Plus 9 quiet tactical puzzle positions (Reinfeld). 14 positions per task per subject: 5 + 5 + 2 + 2.
2. Three subjects representing master / Class A / beginner. Because n = 1 per level, targets are the three trial-1 rank orders and rough magnitudes, not distributions.
3. Copy task: model needs a "glance" cycle: look at the stimulus, store one chunk, place its pieces, look again. Targets: pieces per glance M 2.0 / A 2.8 / B 2.0; between-glance latency M 2.8 / A 3.2 / B 3.5 s; within-glance interval mostly < 2 s, mode <= 0.5 s.
4. Memory task: 5 s exposure, free recall with no time limit, repeated trials until perfect (cap not stated, B took up to 14), random positions not run to criterion. Score = pieces on exact correct square. Targets, middle games first trial: M about 16, A about 8, B about 4 pieces; end games M about 8, A about 7, B about 4; random: all below B's game score (about 3.0/2.5/2.0 estimated from graph, not a reliable text value). Puzzle positions: B 33, A 49, M 81%; original positions 18, 34, 62%. Later-trial gains: M about 4 pieces per trial, A/B 5-6.
5. Timing analog: record time between consecutive placements to 0.1 s. Chunk = run of placements separated by <= 2 s (the paper says 2.5 s gives the same result). In the memory task, drop the reconstruction phase: a pause >= 10 s followed by mostly errors, or a run of >= 5 s pauses with errors. Model needs a latency model with about 2 s per chunk retrieval and a few hundred ms per extra piece in the chunk (Dansereau).
6. Relation statistics to reproduce (Table 6): within-chunk probabilities concentrated in DPC, PCS, DPCS, AP and DC with near-zero none/C/S; between-chunk probabilities near the a-priori (.320 none, .255 C, .148 S for game positions); within-vs-between correlation matrix of Table 7 (within columns r about .89 to each other; within vs between about .02-.23; between vs a-priori .81-.93). The a-priori baseline must be computed over all piece pairs in the position set (CS73a: 30 game positions; GS98 used 100 positions, 26,801 pairs, null .335).
7. Chunk-size targets (Table 8): average pieces per chunk by recall order (M 3.8, 3.0, 2.5, 2.3, 1.9, 1.5, 2.2, 2.0 in middle games; chunks per trial M 7.7 / A 5.7 / B 5.3 middle, 7.6 / 6.4 / 4.2 end). Over 75% of M's chunks are pawn chains, castled-king configurations or same-colour clusters.
8. Known mismatches to expect or model deliberately: master chunk counts rise with skill (not constant 7 +/- 2); later studies (GS98, GS00) find much larger master chunks (largest chunk 10-17 pieces) when physical pick-up limits are removed, so a model matching CS73a should keep chunk sizes small (largest chunk median about 5 for M). Random-position recall should rise slowly with LTM size (about 1 piece per 400 Elo; model 7.77 pieces at 3,646 nodes in G93, up to 23.4% at 70,000 nodes in GS96c), not stay flat.
9. For CHREST-style fits: STM 3 visual chunks (GS00; 7 in G93; GS96c does not state an STM limit in the simulation described), 20 fixations per 5 s, +/- 2 square visual field, 8 s discrimination, 2 s familiarization, 250 ms template-slot fill, 50 ms STM encode, nets of 500 / 10,000 / 300,000 nodes for Class A / Expert / Master.
