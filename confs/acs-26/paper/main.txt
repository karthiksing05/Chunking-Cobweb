                                                            Abstract
     Humans routinely categorise the observations they encounter and combine familiar parts into novel
     composites. The cognitive-systems literature has long studied these two abilities in parallel, one
     under the heading of concept formation and the other under the heading of chunking. Neither line
     of work, taken alone, accounts for the joint exercise that grammatical awareness demands. In this
     paper, we present a postulate-driven theory that treats concept-hood and chunk-hood as two aspects
     of every representation. The theory builds on earlier concept-formation work in the Cobweb family
     and extends it in new directions, adding a compositional vocabulary and matched processes for
     building and unpacking composites. We also describe T RELLIS, an implemented system that in-
     stantiates the theory, and demonstrate its behaviour on a series of synthetic context-free grammars.
     We conclude by discussing related approaches to concepts and chunks and directions for future
     research.


1. Introduction
The cognitive-systems literature has long sought computational accounts of how people organise
experience into recognisable structure. Two lines of work developed in parallel (Langley, 2025):
concept formation groups an observed instance with categories whose members it has never ap-
peared with; chunking combines familiar parts into novel composites. Both are on display when
a listener processes a sentence: individual words are categorised by part of speech and successive
constituents are combined into a partonomic structure. We adopt grammar induction as the empir-
ical domain because it exercises the two abilities together, and because gold parse trees provide an
unambiguous target.
     Concepts are categorical, graded mental structures that summarise regularities shared by a set of
entities and are organised taxonomically (Rosch et al., 1976; Murphy, 2002). The Cobweb family
(Fisher, 1987; Gennari et al., 1989), which we build on, incrementally sorts attribute–value instances
into such a taxonomy. Every variant inherits the same flat substrate: an instance is a set of unrelated
pairs, and the vocabulary contains no name for how the pairs relate. Compositional structure thus
sits outside what any concept-formation system in this lineage can express, and psychological work
on how concepts combine (Osherson & Smith, 1981; Hampton, 1988; Murphy & Medin, 1985)
shows the gap is substantive.
     Chunks, by contrast, denote collections of entities in a specified configuration (Miller, 1956;
Chase & Simon, 1973). Chess masters reproduce briefly-glimpsed configurations far better than
amateurs because they perceive the board in familiar multi-piece chunks (Chase & Simon, 1973),

and language learners aggregate phonemes into syllables and syllables into words as fluency grows.
The idea was formalised in CHREST (Gobet & Lane, 2012), a cognitive architecture that models
expert perception by organising chunks in a discrimination network that grows with experience:
each successful recognition commits the matched configuration to memory as a new chunk node, so
recognition and learning are two faces of the same operation. This tradition descends from EPAM
(Richman, 1991) and runs through much of the grammar-induction literature (Wolff, 1982; Langley
& Stromsten, 2000). But chunking systems share a limitation of their own: a chunk records a
specific configuration of parts, so it cannot on its own recognise a structurally identical composite
whose component words it has never seen. Compositionality is present; categorical generalisation
is not.
     We take the position that a unified account should treat concept-hood and chunk-hood as two
aspects of every representation, and adopt Cobweb as our starting point because it already delivers
the categorical half. What it lacks is a vocabulary for the parts that compose an element and matched
processes for composing children into recognised elements and decomposing them back. Every
element in our theory carries a content description that names its parts and a context description
that names its surroundings; two hierarchies index the same elements through these complementary
lenses.
     We make three contributions. First, we develop a postulate-driven unified theory of concepts and
chunks that extends Cobweb. Second, we describe a concrete system that unifies parsing, genera-
tion, and learning under a single elaboration loop. Third, we report an empirical study on synthetic
grammar-induction tasks characterising how each hierarchy contributes to categorical and compo-
sitional accuracy. We review Cobweb in Section 2, present the extending postulates in Section 3,
describe the implemented system in Section 4, report the evaluation in Section 5, and situate the
work against related literature and open questions in Sections 6 and 7.

2. A Review of Cobweb
Fisher (1987)’s original Cobweb introduced an incremental, unsupervised taxonomic learner over
discrete attribute–value instances. Later variants extended the framework: TRESTLE (MacLellan
et al., 2016) handled relational instances; Cobweb/4L (MacLellan et al., 2022) refined the basic-
level computation; convolutional variants (Lian & MacLellan, 2024) extended reach to perceptual
data; the Matsakis system (Matsakis et al., 2023) showed that carefully crafting the representation
can adapt the substrate to a new domain, an observation Section 4 leans on. Every variant shares the
same four commitments, which we restate below as postulates so the chunking theory of Section 3
can extend them directly.

2.1 Representation
Cobweb draws a sharp line between the raw units of experience it takes in and the categorical
summaries it builds from them.
R1. Knowledge takes two forms: instances and concepts.
R2. An instance is a set of attribute–value pairs describing a single observation.



                                                 2
                           A U NIFIED F RAMEWORK OF C ONCEPTS AND C HUNKS




R3. A concept is a probabilistic summary of a set of instances, expressed as counts over attribute–
     value pairs.
    Concepts in Cobweb hold many instances as aggregated counts and correspond to categorical
generalisations, not exemplars: each concept records, per attribute, a tally of how often each value
was observed among the instances routed through it. Cobweb treats attributes as conditionally inde-
pendent given a concept, with each value distribution smoothed by a uniform prior. This assumption
is what lets us route an element’s content and context descriptions through two separate hierarchies
in Section 3 without changing what happens at any node. The representational vocabulary matters
as much as the sorting mechanism, an observation Matsakis et al. (2023) made concrete by showing
that carefully crafting a phrase-structure representation was enough to adapt Cobweb to grammar
induction; our theory generalises this observation into a permanent representational commitment.

2.2 Organization
Concepts are not stored in isolation; Cobweb arranges them in a structure that supports classifica-
tion.
O1. Concepts are organised in a taxonomic hierarchy rooted at a single concept that summarises
      all observed instances.
O2. Each non-terminal node has two or more children that exclusively partition the instances it
    summarises, and instances themselves reside as the terminal nodes.
    Each non-root node refers to a strict subset of its parent’s instances, so descent is specialisation
rather than composition. Terminal nodes summarise singleton instances, so the same routing ma-
chinery handles intermediate abstractions and individual observations. The taxonomic structure is
what our theory preserves while adding a partonomic dimension on top.

2.3 Performance
The organisation above supports two performance routines that both run over the same hierarchy:
recognition, which places a new instance, and prediction, which reads off unobserved attributes
from the destination.
P1. An instance is sorted down the hierarchy from the root toward a terminal node, with each
      non-terminal routing the instance to the child concept that best fits it under a sorting criterion.
P2. Predictions about unobserved attributes of an instance are read from the concept to which that
     instance is sorted.
    Cobweb’s descent is single-pass: it commits to one child at each level and never backtracks.1
The sorting criterion is category utility (Fisher, 1987), a probabilistic score that rewards a candidate
concept when its instances share attribute values (intra-category coherence) and differ from those of
sibling concepts (inter-category discriminability).

2.4 Learning
Learning in Cobweb is not a separate offline stage but an update rule attached to the same sorting
descent that supports recognition.
L1. Learning is interleaved with performance: each sorted instance is absorbed into the hierarchy
      along the same path that classified it.
L2. At each non-terminal along the path, the system selects among four actions: add the instance
     to an existing child, create a new child, or restructure the hierarchy via merge or split.
    The four-action menu separates two concerns: add and create grow the tree without rearranging
existing structure, while merge and split restructure it. The action selected at every step improves
the same criterion that routed the instance.
    R1–L2 fix Cobweb as an incremental concept-formation substrate: powerful along the cate-
gorical dimension but silent along the compositional one, because its representational vocabulary
contains no name for how the pairs inside an instance relate. Section 3 supplies those missing
pieces.

3. A Unified Theory of Chunking and Categorization
The Cobweb commitments of Section 2 carry directly into the unified theory we propose, and the
postulates below extend them with numbering continued from Section 2 to make the inheritance
explicit. Cobweb’s substrate is missing two things to accommodate compositional elements: a vo-
cabulary for the parts that compose an element, and matched processes for composing children
into recognised elements and decomposing them back. Recent Cobweb variants have moved in
complementary directions; the Cobweb/4L family (MacLellan et al., 2022) recasts the sorting crite-
rion under an information-theoretic objective, whereas our extension holds the objective fixed and
enriches what each instance can express. The postulates lay out the enriched vocabulary, the two hi-
erarchies the theory posits, and the composition and decomposition processes; we use grammatical
structure throughout as a running example because it exercises the categorical and compositional
dimensions in tandem, though the postulates apply to any domain whose experiences carry discrete
relational structure.

3.1 Representation
The theory’s representational vocabulary starts from the same instance/concept distinction as Cob-
web but broadens what an experience can contain, and introduces the compositional element as the
unit that memory organises.
R4. An experience is a collection of instances together with a set of discrete relations between
       them, represented as a set of primitive elements and the local relations that arrange them in
       the experience.
R5. Every element carries two descriptions: a content description, which names the parts that com-
     pose it, and a context description, which names the discrete relations it bears to surrounding
     elements.



                                                 4
                       A U NIFIED F RAMEWORK OF C ONCEPTS AND C HUNKS




R6. Every element is either a primitive or a composite; a composite (also called a chunk) is an
    element built from other primitive or composite elements, and its content description names
    those parts in the same vocabulary the rest of the system uses.
     The two descriptions let the same element be recognised through two independent categori-
cal lenses. Content bears on composition: a composite names its children, and a primitive names
nothing. Context bears on distribution: for a primitive, a small sliding window of the tokens imme-
diately to its left and right in the surface sequence; for a composite, the same window taken over
its outermost positions (so the two boundaries of the composite are what neighbours see). A single
attribute–value bundle would force role and filler into one signal, whereas two indexed descriptions
keep them from interfering.

3.2 Organization
Given elements that each carry two descriptions, memory must record both what an element was
built out of and how each of its descriptions lands in category space.
O3. Primitives and composites are jointly organised in a partonomic hierarchy that records, for
       each composite, the elements it was built from.
O4. The system maintains long-term memory in the form of two distinct taxonomic hierarchies: a
     content hierarchy that organises the content descriptions of R5, and a context hierarchy that
     organises the context descriptions of R5.
     The partonomic hierarchy of O3 holds a parse together and answers “what are the parts?”. The
taxonomic hierarchies of O4 answer “what category does this fall under?”. A context-hierarchy
path groups noun-like tokens together regardless of whether they appeared as primitives or compos-
ites, while a content-hierarchy path groups DET+N-shaped composites together regardless of what
surrounded them, and neither signal leaks into the other.

3.3 Performance
Performance runs in two directions: parsing builds composites from primitives; generation expands
them back. The two subsections that follow share the same template (an incremental step, a candi-
date set, a decision rule, a termination condition).
Parsing.
    Parsing runs bottom-up: it proposes composites over the elements currently on the frontier,
commits the most recognisable one, and grows a partonomic tree until no further composite is
recognised.
P3. Parsing is an incremental process of elaborations on an input experience, and each elaboration
      generates a set of candidate chunks consistent with the current partial parse.
P4. Each candidate chunk is scored against the content and context hierarchies and gated by a
    recognition threshold; the best-scoring candidate that passes the threshold is committed to the
    partonomic parse tree as a new composite element.
P5. Parsing terminates when no remaining candidate passes the recognition threshold.



                                                 5
                             K. S INGARAVADIVELAN AND P. L ANGLEY




    The content score asks whether the composition looks like one the system has consolidated
before; the context score asks whether the surroundings look like ones that have hosted similar
composites. P5 lets the system fail gracefully: when nothing clears the threshold, the parse ends
rather than assert spurious chunks at the boundary of competence.
Generation.
    Generation runs the same loop in reverse: it starts from a recognised seed and expands it into
children until only primitives remain.
P6. Generation is an incremental process of expansions on a composite seed, and each expansion
      produces a candidate leaf consistent with the basic-level concept above the seed’s content
      reference.
P7.   The candidate leaf is sampled from the content hierarchy and conditioned on the context
      hierarchy; the sampled leaf is committed to the partonomic generation tree as the seed’s
      expansion, and its content children (if any) become new seeds.
P8. Generation terminates when every expanded element is a primitive.
    The basic-level constraint of P6 plays the same role for generation that the recognition threshold
plays for parsing. Basic-level concepts, in Rosch’s original sense (Rosch et al., 1976), are the level
of abstraction that people access first when they name or reason about a category (“dog” rather than
“mammal” or “poodle”); operationally, it is the shallowest node in the hierarchy that has already
accumulated enough support to summarise a recurring configuration. Anchoring generation at such
a node keeps sampling from committing to an idiosyncratic deep leaf, and conditioning the sampled
leaf on context (P7) keeps the output distributionally consistent with what the system has already
seen.

3.4 Learning
Learning in our theory is not a separate mechanism attached to parsing or generation. Whenever
a structured experience is available, its content and context instances can be committed to the two
hierarchies exactly the way Cobweb’s sort-and-learn cycle already commits any ordinary instance.
L3. Learning consists of incorporating any structured experience, whether an already-assembled
       parse tree provided as input or one produced during parsing, into the two taxonomic hierar-
       chies.
L4. Each composite element is added to both hierarchies (its content instance to the content hi-
    erarchy and its context instance to the context hierarchy); primitives, which carry no content
    instance, are added to the context hierarchy alone.
L5. Each addition follows the standard sorting-with-learning procedure of the taxonomic hierarchy
     that receives it (Cobweb postulates L1–L2).
    L3–L5 make recognition and learning two faces of a single elaboration step: if we hand the
system a corpus of already-bracketed sentences, it commits their structure straight to memory; if we
hand it raw sentences, parsing supplies the same structure. On “the cat saw a dog”, committing the
cat as an NP routes its content instance to the content hierarchy (recording “DET+N”) and its context
instance to the context hierarchy (recording “sentence start, next to saw”), so each subsequent step

is scored against updated memory. The asymmetric routing of L4 makes the content hierarchy a
record of actually-composed structure rather than surface vocabulary. Section 4 shows a concrete
system built to these commitments.

4. The T RELLIS System for Chunking and Categorization
T RELLIS instantiates the theory of Section 3 on top of the Cobweb framework of Section 2. We
describe the system at the level of design decisions; formulas for the recognition score and threshold
appear in Appendix C.

4.1 Representation
Every element carries two attribute–value descriptions. The content instance names the parts that
compose the element; the context instance names its neighbours. Primitives carry only a context
instance; composites carry both, as required by R5. Figure 1 illustrates one primitive and one
composite drawn from a small parse.
    A composite’s content instance names its left and right children by two kinds of attribute: an
identifier pointing into the context hierarchy (which says what the child is) and a compositional-
complexity tag (a small integer recording how deeply nested the child’s own subtree is). Both values
live in the context hierarchy’s identifier space rather than the surface vocabulary, so the content
hierarchy never sees a surface word: every value a content sort encounters was created by a context
sort of the corresponding child. The complexity tag lets the content hierarchy split composites
whose children share a distributional context but differ in structural depth, such as NP=DET+N
versus S=NP+VP, which look identical at the coarsest granularity.

4.2 Organization
T RELLIS organises long-term memory into two Cobweb taxonomies. The content hierarchy clas-
sifies the content instances of composites; the context hierarchy classifies the context instances of
every element. The two are linked by a single, well-defined interface: a composite’s content in-
stance is built out of context-hierarchy identifiers (Section 4.1), so the content hierarchy never sees
a raw word, and every attribute value it does see was produced by an earlier sort in the context
hierarchy. This link is what lets the performance processes unpack an element along both dimen-
sions in a single elaboration step: parsing scores a candidate composite against its content-hierarchy
destination (does the composition look consolidated?) and its context-hierarchy destination (do the
surroundings look consolidated?) simultaneously, and generation samples a compositional structure
from the content hierarchy while conditioning each child on the context class the structure expects
at that slot. Neither direction has to trade the categorical axis against the compositional one, because
the interface carries information along both at once. Composites with structurally similar children
end up in nearby positions in the content tree, reflecting a shared role (say, as an NP-filling con-
stituent), while a noun and an adjective phrase end up sorted together in the context tree whenever
they appear in comparable neighbourhoods. Figure 2 shows leaf-level subtrees of each hierarchy
and the cross-hierarchy references that stitch them together.

4.3 Performance
T RELLIS realises P3–P8 as two complementary processes: parsing builds chunks incrementally
from an experience, and generation expands chunks back into primitives.

4.3.1 Parsing.
Parsing is a greedy search through the space of possible partonomic parses. It starts with the input’s
primitives on a frontier and repeatedly proposes candidate chunks over adjacent pairs, sorting each
candidate’s content and context instances through their respective hierarchies. Each sort returns
a class-posterior score measuring how well the candidate fits its destination concept relative to
the alternatives (Appendix C); the two scores are summed to produce a single recognition score.
On “the cat saw a dog”, the first step considers the four adjacent primitive pairs and commits the
highest-scoring one (typically the-cat, since DET-N composites are already well-represented) as an
NP composite on the frontier.
    A candidate is admitted only when its context-hierarchy sort has accumulated enough support:
the sort’s path is walked upward from the destination leaf, and if some non-root ancestor’s instance
count exceeds the recognition threshold τ , the candidate passes. This climbing-ancestor gate keeps
the parser from acting on a leaf that has been visited only once or twice, while still allowing rare-
but-consolidated composites to be admitted. Among the admitted candidates, the highest-scoring
one is committed to the partonomic parse tree, its two children leave the frontier, and the next step
runs against memory updated by the learning procedure of Section 4.4. Parsing terminates when no
remaining candidate can be admitted, giving the graceful-failure behaviour P5 asks for.

4.3.2 Generation.
Generation runs the parsing loop in reverse. A generation step begins with a seed (an unexpanded
composite carrying a content-hierarchy leaf identifier) and expands it into two children by sam-

pling from a recombination pool associated with the seed. The pool is the set of committed child
pairs whose parent leaf was routed to a well-supported neighbourhood above the seed’s own leaf;
concretely, this neighbourhood is the shallowest ancestor whose instance count exceeds τ , so pool
membership reflects a recurrent, consolidated substitution class rather than an idiosyncratic single-
instance leaf (P6). Sampling from the neighbourhood, rather than only from the seed’s own leaf, is
what lets the generator produce novel recombinations no single training chunk could have provided.
    Each sampled child is then filtered on the context-hierarchy class the parent expects at that
slot, so the pool cannot emit a wrong-type constituent (P7). If the filter empties the pool for a slot
(typically when a context class has not yet accumulated any compatible chunk), the system falls
back to a wider, less-filtered pool that trades a small commission risk for coverage. Generation halts
when every leaf of the tree is a primitive.

4.4 Learning
Learning in T RELLIS is deliberately brief, and its brevity is where the design pays off. The system
introduces no new learning mechanism: each committed composite and each frontier primitive
is handed to Cobweb’s ordinary sort-and-learn cycle in whichever hierarchies apply. Composites
go into both hierarchies (their content instance to the content hierarchy, their context instance to
the context hierarchy); primitives go only into the context hierarchy, since they carry no content
instance. Because both sorts happen immediately after the commit, candidates produced later in the
same parse are scored against a memory that already reflects the chunks committed earlier. What
makes the setup interesting is the interaction with parsing: every new composite the parser commits
becomes a fresh terminal node in one or both hierarchies, and that terminal is exactly what future
parsing steps use as a category label the next time a structurally similar chunk appears. There is no
separate “chunk creation” subroutine.

5. Evaluation of T RELLIS
The evaluation asks whether T RELLIS does what the postulates promise: recognise gold-standard
chunks in inputs it has not seen and generate new outputs consistent with the grammar it has learned.
Following Langley (1996)’s recommendations for evaluating incremental learners, we identify the
dependent variables, the independent axes our synthetic grammars vary, and the questions the study
is meant to answer.
Dependent variables. We follow Langley & Stromsten (2000)’s framing of grammar-induction
competence, which decomposes errors into two complementary categories. An omission error is
gold structure the learner failed to produce (a false negative); a commission error is structure the
learner produced that was not in the gold data (a false positive). On the parsing side we report
the parser’s bracket accuracy on held-out gold parses: a parse that misses a gold bracket incurs an
omission, and a parse that asserts a bracket the gold parse does not contain incurs a commission,
so the reported score falls when either kind of error is made. On the generation side we report
the generator’s grammaticality rate, the fraction of sampled sentences the source grammar accepts;
this captures the commission axis directly (a sampled sentence outside the grammar is a commission
error), while omission on the generation side is not directly interpretable since the generator samples
freely rather than trying to enumerate the grammar.
Independent variables. Two axes, matched to the two dimensions our theory keeps separate.
First, the number of non-terminal categories, holding the lexicon fixed; each new non-terminal is
a chunk type the content hierarchy must distinguish. Second, the number of terminals per part-of-
speech class, holding productions fixed; thinning per-word support pushes the context hierarchy to
generalise across rare surface forms.

 5.4 Summary
 The parser stays within an omission/commission budget of 0.05 to 0.09 across every condition,
 recovering gold brackets at accuracy between 0.91 and 1.00 (Q1). The generator’s commission rate
 stays under 0.09 everywhere, producing grammatical outputs at rates between 0.91 and 1.00 (Q2).
 Both abilities degrade gracefully as non-terminals are added, with parsing degrading slightly faster
 than generation (Q3), and both are largely insensitive to lexicon size (Q4). The asymmetry is what
 Section 3 predicts: the categorical dimension carries the load at parse time (it is the dimension the
 recognition threshold judges), while the distributional dimension is absorbed almost entirely by the
 context hierarchy’s generalisation across surface forms.

 6. Related Work
 Several bodies of work cover some but not all of what T RELLIS delivers: unified accounts of cate-
 gorical and compositional memory, incremental parsing and inverse generation under a single learn-
 ing loop, discrete role–filler binding, and unsupervised acquisition of constituent structure.
     The closest precursor is Langley (2025)’s essay arguing that a unified theory should treat concept-
 hood and chunk-hood as two aspects of every representation. That essay proposes commitments
 any such system would need to satisfy but does not commit to an implementation; our theory is one
 concrete instance of it. The Matsakis system (Matsakis et al., 2023) extends Cobweb with iterative
 additions but does not draw a content–context distinction. The convolutional Cobweb variant (Lian
 & MacLellan, 2024) pairs a chunking-style construction process with a classification-style hierarchy
 but fixes the construction by architecture and operates on images rather than the sequential parto-
 nomic structure that drives our parsing loop. The CHREST family (Gobet & Lane, 2012; Richman,


                                                     13
                             K. S INGARAVADIVELAN AND P. L ANGLEY




1991), originally built to model chess-expert memory, organises chunks in a discrimination network:
an internal decision tree whose nodes acquire test-based subclassifications as new chunks are recog-
nised. This machinery captures acquisition and rapid recognition well, but its chunks match input
by exact lookup rather than by generalising across structurally similar instances, which is exactly
the limitation the two-hierarchy organisation is designed to lift.
     Adjacent to the cognitive-systems tradition, the connectionist literature asks what a composi-
tional representation must support. Smolensky (1990) developed tensor-product representations, in
which a role (say “subject”) and a filler (say “the cat”) are bound together as the outer product of
their vectors, so a structured representation is a sum of role-filler bindings recoverable later. Plate
(1995)’s holographic reduced representations obtain a similar effect with circular convolution as a
compressed binding operator. Our content/context split reads as a discrete counterpart: two indexed
descriptions keep role and filler from interfering while remaining ordinary Cobweb instances that
need no vector algebra.
     The problem of inducing a grammar from unannotated sentences asks for the same combination
of compositional structure and categorical generalisation our theory addresses. Wolff (1982) intro-
duced an early system that merges frequent adjacent pairs and generalises the resulting categories;
its two operations prefigure our two hierarchies, though they are not maintained as separate struc-
tures. Langley & Stromsten (2000) introduced GRIDS, which incrementally hypothesises grammar
productions and evaluates candidate grammars by a minimum-description-length (MDL) criterion
that trades the size of the grammar against how compactly it encodes the training corpus. T REL -
LIS borrows the omission/commission decomposition from that same paper. These systems treat
the categorical and compositional aspects as a single optimisation rather than as two dimensions of
memory, and correspondingly lack a single substrate from which both parsing and generation fall
out under one elaboration loop.
     A third body of work asks whether local context alone can drive syntactic categorisation. Dis-
tributional embeddings cluster words that occur in similar contexts, and probes over contextualised
models recover POS tags and constituency structure from intermediate layers. Against the concepts–
chunks dichotomy, these models sit closer to concept formation: they extract a context-driven cate-
gorical signal much like the one our context hierarchy maintains. They are further from chunking:
constituent structure is present latently but not laid out as a manipulable partonomy, and behavioural
tests on systematic generalisation continue to expose compositional failures. Our theory pairs the
context-driven signal with an equally explicit content-driven one, so composition is a first-class
property of memory.

7. Concluding Remarks
We have presented a postulate-driven theory that combines the categorical commitments of concept
formation with the compositional commitments of chunking: every element carries a content and
a context description, organized across two Cobweb hierarchies, with one elaboration loop turning
them into parsing, generation, and incremental learning. T RELLIS recovers gold chunks at high
precision and recall and generates near-perfectly grammatical sentences. Chunks and contexts sit
in mutual dependence: chunks are identifiable only because their contexts are similar, and contexts


                                                  14
                       A U NIFIED F RAMEWORK OF C ONCEPTS AND C HUNKS




are informative only because they constrain the chunks that fill them. This explains the empirical
asymmetry between parsing and generation: parsing fails when context support has not cleared the
threshold, while generation succeeds because every chunk in the content hierarchy was placed there
by a parse that had already cleared it.
     Two future endeavors follow. Unsupervised chunk learning is the most pressing: T RELLIS
still relies on a tuned recognition threshold τ , and earlier statistical approaches (MDL grammars,
mutual-information chunkers that merge adjacent tokens whenever their co-occurrence exceeds a
cutoff) struggled to say what makes a chunk good once superficial recurrence had been exhausted.
The theory points toward a chunk-intrinsic answer: a chunk is good to the extent that it is stable
across contexts, reusable as a part of larger chunks, and predictive of its neighbours. The second en-
deavor is to push the substrate beyond sentences: upward into paragraphs and discourse units, down-
ward into letters and phonemes; the single left/right relation used here should generalize to broader
discrete-relation vocabularies (gridlike neighbourhoods, temporal sequences) without changing the
postulates.

Appendix C. Implementation Formulas
Recognition score.      The Cobweb log-probability of an instance i under a concept C is, by R3,
                                 X                                             nC,a,v + αa
              log P (i | C) =             log P (v | C, a),   P (v | C, a) =                ,
                                                                               nC,a + Va αa
                                (a,v)∈i

where nC,a,v is the count of value v for attribute a at concept C, Va is the value-vocabulary size,
and αa is the uniform-prior smoothing (R3). Candidates are ranked not by this raw log-probability
but by the class posterior it induces: sorting instance i into a hierarchy selects some class C, and
the score is log P (C | i) = log P (i | C) + log P (C) − log P (i). Normalising out the instance
marginal makes the score discriminative, so a non-constituent that fits nowhere in particular scores
low. Content and context scores are the two posteriors sc and sx ; the ranking key is sx + sc , with
sx breaking ties.
Recognition threshold (climbing ancestor). An element’s path in the context hierarchy is walked
upward from its destination leaf. The candidate is admitted when a non-root ancestor has accumu-
lated an instance count exceeding the recognition threshold τ ; otherwise it fails. Sharing this gate
between primitives and composites is what gives the parsing loop a single notion of “mature repre-
sentation” across both layers.
Generation anchor and chunk replay. The same climbing-ancestor notion realises the level P6
samples from: the anchor above a leaf is the shallowest ancestor whose count exceeds τ . Dur-

                       A U NIFIED F RAMEWORK OF C ONCEPTS AND C HUNKS




ing training every committed chunk is recorded as a tuple (parent-leaf, left-child, right-child), in-
dexed by both its parent-leaf identifier and that leaf’s maturity-cut ancestor. Generation samples a
sentence-root chunk as the seed and recurses on each child: primitives are emitted directly; compos-
ites are expanded by sampling a chunk from the maturity-level pool, restricted to those whose own
context class matches what the parent expects at that slot (P7 conditioning). The maturity-level pool
makes recombinations no single leaf could produce reachable, while context conditioning keeps the
wider pool from emitting a wrong-type constituent.
Content-instance encoding. Each content attribute value is a small bag over concept identifiers
rather than a single symbol, preserving the concept’s own uncertainty rather than forcing a commit
to its most-likely value. A visible complexity tag on each child lets category utility separate compos-
ites whose children share a distributional context but differ in structural depth (NP=DET+N versus
S= NP+ VP, which share the same right-hand-side signature at the coarsest granularity). Because
the context hierarchy restructures as new instances are absorbed, content references can dangle over
time; T RELLIS canonicalises each reference to a recent ancestor of its target leaf and rewrites stored
instances whenever the context tree restructures.
